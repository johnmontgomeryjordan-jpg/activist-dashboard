Purpose

An early-warning screen that flags public companies likely to attract an activist investor — before the activist surfaces — so FGS can reach the company first, with a specific, evidence-backed point of view.

By the time a stake is disclosed, the company is already taking calls and the field is crowded. The edge is getting there early. This tool ranks the S&P 1500 every morning on the traits activists actually screen for — measured against each company's industry peers — and turns the strongest matches into a ready-to-use pitch: the thesis, the talking points, the supporting numbers, and who to call.

Who it's for

An internal new-business tool for the FGS team — partners and team members alike. It's a lead-generation and preparation aid, used inside the firm.

What it is — and isn't

  • It's a first-pass screen, not the last word. It tells you where to look; you confirm the story in the evidence and your own research before acting on it.
  • It's not a replacement for FactSet, Diligent, Bloomberg or a dedicated activism database — it points you at names worth a closer look in those tools, faster and for free.
  • It measures how strongly a company fits the profile activists target. A high rating is a strong match, not a guarantee a campaign will happen (more on that under Limitations).

Methodology

The universe

Roughly the S&P 1500, screened to companies above a ~$1B market cap. Delisted, deregistered or long-dormant names are dropped automatically and kept out of the peer benchmarks.

The core idea

Activists look for a recognizable setup: a company that is underperforming, for a reason that's fixable, with a lever someone can pull (capital to return, costs to cut, a board to refresh, a business to sell). The tool measures how closely each company matches that setup and ranks the strongest matches.

How the rating works

Every trait is measured relative to a company's industry peers (grouped by 2-digit SIC code), not on an absolute basis — because the same number means different things in different industries. A 12% operating margin is weak for software and strong for a grocer; "cheap" for a bank isn't "cheap" for a chipmaker. Comparing each company to its own peer set is what makes a flag meaningful.

Each triggered signal contributes points weighted by how severe it is — how deep in the peer tail, how cheap, how far returns lag. The points sum to a 0–92 activist-target profile index, shown as a band: 75+ Severe 50–74 High 25–49 Elevated <25 Moderate It is capped below 100 by design, so it never reads as a certainty — it's a measure of fit, not odds.

The signals

Valuation gap — cheap vs peers (low price-to-book); cheap on EV/EBITDA vs peers.
Capital allocation — cash-heavy balance sheet; under-levered; goodwill-heavy (an acquisition-overpayment / impairment-risk proxy).
Operating performance — low operating margin vs peers; low return on assets; weak or shrinking revenue; bloated SG&A.
Shareholder returns — 1-year price return lagging the S&P; multi-year (3/5-year) price return lagging the S&P (a structural underperformer).
Governance red flags — classified/staggered board; poison pill; dual-class super-voting stock; CEO pay rising while the stock lags (pay-for-performance gap, from the proxy).
Peer benchmarking — trails the company's own self-selected compensation peer group (the peers it named in its proxy).
Shareholder support — weak say-on-pay vote at the last annual meeting.
Insider activity — a cluster of open-market insider selling (Form 4).
Event catalysts — recent CEO/exec departure, earnings miss, impairment, layoffs, leadership change, a negative press headline, or a sharp stock drop on a leadership-change 8-K.

Every signal on a company's profile carries an evidence card with the underlying number, the SEC math behind it, the peer cutoff, the period, and a link to the source filing.

Sanity checks & guards

Raw peer-percentile signals can be misleading on their own, so each is filtered through a set of guards that ask "is this actually an activist lever, or an accounting artifact?" These keep the score honest and the thesis credible:

  • Profitability gate — the operating-improvement signals (low margin, bloated SG&A, low return on assets) only fire for a company that is actually profitable. You expand a thin-but-positive margin by cutting costs; you can't cost-cut your way out of a loss. A negative margin is reinvestment, amortization, or distress — captured by other signals, not a "margin turnaround."
  • One-time-charge guard — a goodwill impairment or divestiture can crush a single quarter (operating income near break-even, but the bottom line deeply negative). The screen detects that below-the-line distortion and suppresses the false "loses money" signals.
  • Bank & insurer carve-out — for financials, cash and investments are regulatory reserves / float, leverage is structural, and there's no industrial "operating margin" or EBITDA, so those signals are turned off. What still applies — and is what real bank activists use — is the discount to (tangible) book, price underperformance, return-on-assets vs. peers, M&A history, and governance.
  • Multi-year-winner guard — a stock that has beaten the market over three years isn't a "sustained underperformer." A one-year dip on a multi-year winner is profit-taking, not a campaign setup, so its soft value / operating / balance-sheet signals and the sale-candidate flag are suppressed (it keeps governance, pay and event flags, which apply even to winners).
  • Actionability discounts — founder- or dual-class-controlled companies (where insiders control the vote) and deep cash-burners (falling knives) are discounted, since a campaign is far harder or the value is already impaired.
  • Headline precision — a "negative press headline" must be both genuinely negative/distress and actually name the company, so routine items (conference transcripts, "top movers" columns, articles about other companies) don't get flagged.
  • Most-recent data — fundamentals always read the company's current SEC filing, even when a company has switched the XBRL tags it reports under (which previously could freeze a name on years-old figures).

Active situations (already engaged)

When an activist has already shown up, the name moves off the proactive shortlist into Active Situations at one of three confidence tiers: Confirmed (an authoritative SEC filing on record — a 13D, a contested proxy, or dissident soliciting materials), Reported (a known activist named in the press alongside the company, or an exempt solicitation — these are sometimes a serious activist but often an ESG/gadfly filer, so they're held at lower confidence), or Manual (tagged by a team member). These are tracked for awareness — they're usually past the point of a proactive pitch, though a fresh filing can be an urgent defense lead.

Where the data comes from — all public

Everything here is built from publicly available data — SEC filings and free market-data tiers. There's no private, paid, or non-public information in the screen.

  • SEC XBRL — fundamentals: revenue, margins, SG&A, returns, cash, debt, goodwill, D&A.
  • SEC EDGAR — 8-K / 10-K / 10-Q events, Form 4 insider trades, 8-K Item 5.07 vote results, DEF 14A proxies (governance, CEO pay, peer group), and a 13D / contested-proxy sweep.
  • Finnhub — market cap, price-to-book, P/E, 1-yr return, earnings calendar, insider sentiment.
  • Twelve Data — daily price history and multi-year (3/5-yr) price returns.
  • Financial Modeling Prep — company profile and IR/contact details.
  • GDELT (free, real-time global news) — broad activist / distress headlines for the Live feed; Finnhub company-news — per-company headlines shown on each profile.
  • Claude (Haiku) — re-voices the thesis prose, strictly limited to the facts above.

The AI thesis layer

The pitch thesis and talking points are first assembled deterministically from the computed numbers, then re-voiced by Claude Haiku for readability — constrained to only the facts we computed, never adding new claims. The deterministic evidence cards remain the source of truth, and a "✦ AI-polished" tag marks where the AI voice is shown.

How often it updates

  • Every 30 minutes — news, filings, market cap & valuation refresh, and scores recompute.
  • Daily at 6 AM ET — full SEC fundamentals, governance & CEO pay, insider, votes, the universe-wide activist sweep, multi-year price returns, price charts, the AI thesis, and the emailed pitch kit. The "lead of the day" rotates on this 6 AM build, so the site is fresh each morning and captures the prior night's filings and headlines.

Metrics & terms

Each metric below is what it measures and, more importantly, why an activist cares about it — i.e., why it earns a place in the screen.

Valuation & balance sheet

  • Price-to-book (P/B) — share price vs. the company's net asset value. Why it matters: a low P/B says the market values the company near or below its own assets — the market doesn't believe management will create value from what it has. It's the simplest "this is undervalued and fixable" entry point.
  • EV/EBITDA — enterprise value (market cap + debt − cash) against operating earnings. Why it matters: the standard multiple buyers and peers are judged on. A low multiple versus peers is the cleanest "you're cheap" argument and screens for sale / break-up candidates.
  • Cash / assets — how much of the balance sheet is cash. Why it matters: idle cash is the #1 activist lever — the push to return it via buybacks, dividends, or a special dividend writes itself.
  • Debt / assets — how levered the company is. Why it matters: an under-levered balance sheet is "lazy capital"; activists argue for borrowing to buy back stock or fund growth.
  • Goodwill / assets — how much of the balance sheet is the premium paid in past acquisitions. Why it matters: a lot of goodwill means a lot of deals — it flags possible overpayment and impairment risk, and supports a "stop the empire-building" argument.

Operating performance

  • Operating margin — operating income ÷ revenue, vs. peers. Why it matters: below-peer margins are a cost story — the classic "there's fat to cut" / margin-expansion campaign.
  • Return on assets (ROA) — profit generated per dollar of assets. Why it matters: weak returns say the asset base isn't working, supporting strategy- or management-change pressure.
  • Revenue growth — top-line trend. Why it matters: a stalling or shrinking top line invites calls for a strategic review, a break-up, or a sale.
  • SG&A % of revenue — overhead as a share of sales. Why it matters: bloated overhead is the most concrete, quantifiable cost-cut target an activist can name.

Shareholder returns

  • Price return, 1 / 3 / 5-yr vs. the S&P 500 — share-price return (excludes dividends) against the market. Why it matters: sustained underperformance is the emotional core of every campaign — "the board has destroyed value." The multi-year view separates one bad year from a structural problem, which is the more damning case.

Governance & pay

  • Classified / staggered board — directors elected in classes over multiple years. Why it matters: it slows a proxy fight, so it's both an entrenchment red flag and a reform target.
  • Poison pill — a shareholder-rights plan that makes accumulating a large stake costly. Why it matters: it blocks stake-building and is a lightning rod for "just vote no" pressure.
  • Dual-class stock — share classes with unequal votes. Why it matters: insiders keep control regardless of economics — a governance flashpoint.
  • CEO pay-for-performance — the trend in CEO pay against the stock. Why it matters: pay rising while the stock falls is the most relatable, headline-ready attack an activist has.
  • Say-on-pay support — the annual shareholder vote on executive pay. Why it matters: weak support means shareholders are already unhappy — a recognized pre-activism warning sign.
  • Self-selected compensation peer group — the peers the board itself named in its proxy. Why it matters: showing a company lags the peers it chose is more credible than any sector comparison — "you underperform even your own benchmark."

Insider activity & events

  • Insider selling (Form 4) — open-market sales by officers and directors. Why it matters: a cluster of insiders selling is management voting with their feet.
  • Exec-change stock reaction — the 1-day move vs. the market when a leadership-change 8-K hits. Why it matters: a sharp drop is the market saying it's lost confidence in the transition — a timing-based opening.

Filings you'll see referenced

  • 13D — filed when an investor crosses 5% ownership with intent to influence; the clearest sign an activist has actually engaged.
  • DEF 14A (proxy statement) — the annual filing with board, governance, executive pay, and the self-selected peer group.
  • 8-K — material-event filing. Item 5.02 = executive/director changes; Item 5.07 = shareholder vote results.
  • 10-K / 10-Q — annual / quarterly financial reports (the source of the fundamentals).
  • Form 4 — insider open-market buys and sells.

Using it

A quick tour of how to work the tool day to day.

Start on the Daily Pitch Kit

The home page is the morning brief: the lead of the day (the highest-conviction name, on a no-repeat rotation so it changes daily), a few more proactive targets, and the overnight headlines and filings. It refreshes every morning, so it's a standing place to start the day.

Open a company profile

Click any name for the full work-up: the "why it's a potential target" thesis and three talking points, the evidence grouped by pillar (each card shows the number, the SEC math, the peer cutoff, and a link to the source filing), financials with meaning (each metric flagged as a vulnerability or an opportunity vs. peers), the peer analysis tab, returns vs. the S&P, governance flags, and who to reach (IR / Comms contacts pulled from filings).

Track and triage

Use the Dashboard to sort and filter the full ranked list. Star a name to add it to the shared Watchlist. Active situations lists companies where an activist has already engaged (by confidence tier); you can add or remove one by hand. Export the list to CSV to work it elsewhere.

Read it the right way

Treat the rating as "worth a look," not a verdict. Open the evidence, sanity-check the numbers against the source filings and your own tools, and use the thesis as a starting draft — not a finished pitch. The Feedback tab is there to flag a misfire or a missing name.

Limitations

This is a screen, and screens are wrong sometimes. Here's why it misfires, so you can read a flag with the right amount of skepticism. The short version: it measures a profile, from public, point-in-time data, on free tiers — and each of those is a source of error.

It scores the profile, not the intent

The tool measures how much a company looks like a target. It can't see whether an activist actually will engage. A company can match the profile perfectly and never draw a campaign — because insiders control the vote, it just settled with an activist, it's too small or illiquid for the funds, or the board is already acting. And an activist can target a name that doesn't screen, driven by a catalyst or thesis the data can't capture. Fit is a strong prior, not a forecast.

It can't tell "fixable" from "broken" or "in decline"

The screen sees that a company is cheap and underperforming; it can't see why. Three flavors look identical in the numbers but are very different in reality, and need your judgment:

  • Catastrophe / litigation overhang. A name can screen cheap because of a massive lawsuit or contingent liability, not fixable underperformance — e.g. a utility buried in wildfire-tort liability. That's a distressed/special-situation play, not an activist campaign.
  • Accounting / litigation catalyst. A restatement or securities class action can drive a de-rate and governance flags without an activist in sight — the story is the accounting, not a campaign (yet).
  • Secular decline. A "fallen angel" can be a falling knife — a quality business whose end-market is in structural decline (e.g. a device maker facing a new drug class). Activists often avoid these, which is part of why a strong profile can sit without a campaign.

In each case the rating is doing its job (the profile is a match) — but the qualitative read is yours. Planned roadmap signals would flag the first two automatically.

Point-in-time accounting can distort a quarter

Fundamentals come from the most recent quarterly filing. A one-time event — an impairment, a restructuring charge, a legal settlement — can crush a single quarter's margin or return on assets and make a perfectly healthy company screen as distressed. The evidence card shows the period, so a quick look usually reveals when a number is a blip rather than a trend.

Peer grouping is approximate

Companies are compared within their 2-digit industry code. That's a reasonable proxy, but it's coarse: a conglomerate, a holding company, or an unusual business model can be lumped with peers it doesn't really resemble, which skews where it lands in the percentile and can over- or under-state a signal.

Free-tier data has gaps

Running entirely on free, public data means some inputs are partial: the earnings calendar and say-on-pay vote parsing don't resolve for every name, multi-year returns and price charts are pulled for the top-ranked set rather than the whole universe, and a self-selected peer group only resolves when enough of those peers are in coverage. Missing data shows up as a missing signal, not an "all clear" — absence of a flag isn't proof of safety.

News and filing matching isn't perfect

Headlines and filings are matched to companies by name and ticker, and there's a natural lag. It can miss a relevant item or, occasionally, associate one with the wrong company — so the news/filings feed is a prompt to look, not a vetted record.

Bottom line

Use a flag as a lead to investigate, not a conclusion. Open the evidence, check the source filings and your own tools, and form your own judgment before you act on it. It's an internal tool to point attention in the right direction — the analysis is still yours.

Roadmap

The tool runs today on entirely free, publicly available data — which is remarkable for what it does, and also the source of most of its current limits. The biggest improvements all come from moving past those free tiers. Here's where it stands now versus what it could become.

What more investment could unlock

  • Full-universe market data. Professional pricing and valuation would cover every name with live, multi-year data — not just the top-ranked set it pulls today — and lift the daily limits that currently cap how much it can refresh.
  • Better, faster news. High-quality financial news is largely paywalled and hard to reach from a free site, which is why the current feed is thin. A paid source would mean earlier, broader, cleaner signal — often the first hint a situation is developing.
  • Deeper signals. Richer pay-versus-performance, guidance-versus-delivery, and segment / break-up analysis to sharpen the case on each name.
  • Wider distribution & reliability. Team-wide automated delivery, individual logins, backups, and alerts when a company jumps into a higher band.
  • Beyond internal use. Commercially-licensed data is also what would let the tool move past an internal-only setting down the road.

Planned new signals (deeper coverage)

These came out of our lead-quality audits and would close the remaining judgment-call gaps:

  • Litigation / contingent-liability overhang. Flag names that are cheap because of a major lawsuit or large reserve (e.g. a utility with wildfire-tort liability) so they read as distressed / special-situations, not clean activist leads — addressing the biggest "fixable vs. catastrophe" gap under Limitations.
  • Restatement / material-weakness signal. Detect 8-K Item 4.02 ("non-reliance on previously issued financials") and material-weakness disclosures — one of the strongest activist catalysts, currently uncaptured.
  • Measured acquisition reaction. An event study on the actual stock move when a deal is announced (8-K Item 1.01/2.01), upgrading today's inferred "de-rated since a goodwill-heavy acquisition" to a measured "the market dropped the stock X% on the announcement."
  • Bank-specific signal pack. Price-to-tangible-book, excess capital vs. a target CET1 ratio, and the efficiency ratio — the metrics real bank activists use — so banks are scored on what actually applies to them rather than just being carved out.
  • Secular-threat / quality-de-rater nuance. Distinguish a fallen-but-viable business from one whose end-market is in structural decline (a multi-year-high de-rate plus an end-market signal), so "falling knives" are flagged as such.
  • Consistency & coverage tidy-ups. Extend the multi-year-winner guard to the return-on-assets signal; and tighten news auto-routing so a known activist named in the press reliably moves a name into Active Situations.

The single highest-leverage upgrade is professional market data — it removes the free-tier limits behind most of what's described under Limitations.
